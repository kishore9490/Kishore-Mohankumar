# 15 · Sales Pitch & Messaging

> **Note:** the source brief was truncated mid-sentence in this section. The one-line pitch below completes it as intended; reconcile against the original wording if it differs.

## 15.1 The one-line pitch

> **BID Trust is an enterprise verification and due-diligence platform that verifies the businesses and people you do business with — against your own policy, through authorized data sources, with an auditable record you can defend to a regulator, a customer or a court.**

**Shorter, for a corridor conversation:**
> *"We verify the businesses and people you work with — your policy, our infrastructure, one auditable record."*

**Shortest, for a WhatsApp reply:**
> *"Vendor and workforce verification, automated and auditable."*

## 15.2 The positioning statement

> For **mid-market and enterprise organizations** that must verify vendors, contractors and employees,
> **BID Trust** is an **enterprise trust and due-diligence platform**
> that **turns weeks of manual document-chasing into a policy-driven, auditable verification record**,
> unlike **point KYC APIs, BGV agencies or vendor management systems**,
> because it is **one configurable engine across every entity type, every relationship and every industry — with the record kept current, not filed away.**

## 15.3 The 60-second pitch

> Every company repeatedly asks the same question about a different counterparty: *can I safely do business with this entity?*
>
> Right now you answer it with emails, spreadsheets, a shared drive, and two or three different agencies. It takes two to four weeks per vendor. Nobody owns the record. And when your auditor asks how you verified a supplier eighteen months ago, somebody goes searching through an inbox.
>
> BID Trust replaces that. You define your verification policy once — what a critical supplier must prove, what a contractor must prove, what a candidate must prove. You add your vendors. They complete a secure verification. We orchestrate the checks through authorized sources and give you a verified record with an audit trail.
>
> Then we keep watching. If a vendor's GST registration is cancelled, a director is disqualified, or an ISO certificate expires — you find out that week, not at the next audit.
>
> One platform for vendors, contractors and employees. Your policy, not ours.

## 15.4 The demo script

A demo that opens with a settings screen loses. Open with the problem, in the customer's own language, using their own vendors' names — a demo populated with *their* suppliers converts several times better than one with placeholder data.

| Min | Beat | What to show |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | **Their problem, restated** | No product. Repeat back their onboarding timeline and their audit pain. Get agreement before showing anything. |
| 3–6 | **The policy** | Build "Critical Supplier" live. *"This is your standard, encoded once."* |
| 6–9 | **The campaign** | Add 10 vendors — use their real vendor names. Send invitations. |
| 9–13 | **The vendor experience** | Switch to the vendor's view. Show how little effort it takes. **This is the most important part of the demo** — the buyer's real fear is that their vendors won't do it. |
| 13–17 | **The result** | The verified record: status, level, assessment factors, evidence, dates, audit trail. |
| 17–20 | **The exception** | Show a vendor that fails — cancelled GST. *"You'd have found this in month nine. You found it on day one."* |
| 20–24 | **Monitoring** | An alert firing on a live vendor. *"This is what you have no way of doing today."* |
| 24–27 | **The audit trail** | Export the record. *"This is what you hand your auditor."* |
| 27–30 | **The pilot** | 10 vendors, 4 weeks, agreed success criteria, defined price. |

**Demo rules:**
1. Never demo a feature they did not ask about.
2. Show the exception case — a demo where everything passes is not credible to anyone who has done this work.
3. Show the vendor side. Always.
4. End with a specific, small, priced next step.

## 15.5 The discovery questions

The sale is made here, not in the demo.

**Scale and process**
1. How many new vendors do you onboard in a year?
2. What does your current verification process look like, step by step?
3. How many days from first contact to approved in your system?
4. Who does that work? How much of their week is it?

**Pain**
5. What happens when a vendor turns out to have a problem after onboarding?
6. Has that happened? *(Listen carefully. This is the real budget trigger.)*
7. When was your last audit? What did they ask about vendor diligence?
8. How would you answer if an auditor asked how you verified a specific vendor two years ago?

**Currency**
9. How do you know a vendor's registrations are still valid today?
10. What percentage of your vendor base has been re-verified in the last 12 months? *(The answer is usually near zero, and it usually lands hard.)*

**Scope**
11. Do you have contract workers on site? How is their identity and certification checked?
12. How is candidate background verification handled today, and by whom?

**Decision**
13. If you wanted to fix this, who else would need to be involved?
14. Is there budget for this, or would it need to be created?
15. What would have to be true for you to run a pilot in the next 30 days?

Question 10 is the strongest single question in the list. Most organizations genuinely do not know the answer, and realizing that is often the moment the deal becomes real.

## 15.6 Objection handling

**"We already have a KYC/verification API."**
> That gives you data. It doesn't give you a policy, a workflow, a vendor-facing process, an evidence trail or monitoring. Most customers keep their API — we can route through it. What they buy from us is everything around it. Who currently decides which checks run for which vendor, and where is that recorded?

**"We use a BGV agency."**
> Keep them if they work — they can be one of our providers. What an agency doesn't give you is a policy engine, a live record, expiry monitoring, or one platform across vendors, contractors and employees. And you probably can't see, right now, how many of your verified employees have a certification expiring this quarter.

**"Our vendors won't complete this."**
> Fair — it's the right thing to worry about, and it's the metric we hold ourselves to. Our target is over 80% completion within seven days. In the pilot we'll measure it and show you. We also assist vendors directly by phone where needed. And vendors that already have a BID profile complete in minutes because they reuse what's verified.

**"This is expensive."**
> Compared with what? Let's do the arithmetic: your team spends X hours per vendor at Y cost, across Z vendors. Then the cost of one bad vendor — a payment to a fraudulent account, a compliance finding, a stop-work order. We're not competing with zero; we're competing with a manual process that's already costing you money and isn't producing an auditable record.

**"We'll build this internally."**
> You can build the workflow. You can't build the provider relationships, the normalization layer, the evidence architecture, or the network of already-verified entities. And you'd own the compliance obligations of handling this data yourself. Most teams that try this end up with a document-collection portal and still call the same agencies.

**"How do we know your verification is accurate?"**
> Every result shows its source, method, timestamp and scope. Nothing is a black box. Where a source is uncertain — court records with common names, for example — we show it as a potential match requiring review, never as a fact. And we say when we don't know something, rather than guessing. Would you like to see how an ambiguous result is presented?

**"What if you're wrong about a vendor?"**
> We report verified evidence, not conclusions. We never tell you a company is trustworthy — we tell you what was verified, against what source, on what date. The decision stays yours, and the record shows exactly what you relied on. There's also a dispute process, and any entity can challenge a finding about itself.

**"Is this government-approved?"**
> No, and we're careful never to imply otherwise. We're a private platform that verifies information against government registries and other authorized sources through legitimate channels. We're not an arm of the state and we don't issue any official status.

**"What about data privacy?"**
> It's the core of the architecture, not a policy document. Minimum necessary disclosure — you get what you need to make and defend a decision, not everything about the vendor. Every disclosure beyond that requires the entity's specific consent. We're building for DPDP compliance and toward ISO 27001 and SOC 2. Our security documentation is available for your review.

**"Can you give us everything about the vendor?"**
> No — and that's deliberate. Tell me what decision you're trying to make, and I'll show you what we disclose for it. Over-collecting personal data creates obligations for *you* as well as for us. Buyers who over-collect are the ones who get findings at audit.

## 15.7 Messaging by buyer

| Buyer | Lead with | Prove with | Never lead with |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Procurement** | Speed and throughput | Days saved per vendor | Compliance |
| **Compliance / Risk** | Auditability and policy enforcement | The audit trail export | Speed |
| **HR** | Turnaround and candidate experience | Time-to-clear, candidate visibility | Vendor management |
| **Operations / EHS** | Worker credential control and site safety | The expiry dashboard, blocked-access example | Procurement workflow |
| **Finance** | Payment fraud prevention | Bank account ownership verification | Everything else |
| **CXO** | Third-party risk exposure across the organization | The single view across vendors, workers and employees | Feature detail |

## 15.8 Proof points to build in year 1

The pitch is currently assertion. Convert each into evidence as fast as possible:

| Claim | Proof needed |
|---|---|
| "Weeks become days" | Measured before/after from three pilots |
| ">80% vendor completion" | Real completion data across campaigns |
| "Catches problems you'd miss" | Documented exceptions found in pilots — *the single most persuasive artifact BID can own* |
| "Audit-ready" | An auditor or CA firm confirming the record is sufficient |
| "Works across industries" | Reference customers in three different sectors |
| "Secure" | ISO 27001, penetration test summary, security questionnaire pack |

The second row deserves emphasis: **a real, documented case where BID caught something a customer's existing process missed** — a cancelled registration, a forged certificate, a mismatched bank account — is worth more than every slide in the deck. Instrument pilots specifically to capture these, and get permission to tell the story.

## 15.9 Words to use and avoid

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Verified against | Guaranteed |
| Evidence, record, audit trail | Score, rating |
| Potential match, requires review | Criminal, fraudulent, blacklisted |
| Authorized sources | Government approved, official |
| Assessment | Credit score |
| Insufficient evidence | High risk *(when data is merely absent)* |
| Your policy | Our standard |
| Helps you decide | Tells you who to trust |

These are not stylistic preferences. Several are direct legal exposures ([§09.6](09-risk-assessment.md#96-language-discipline)), and the discipline has to hold in every deck, email and demo — including the ones sent at 11pm by someone chasing a quarter.
