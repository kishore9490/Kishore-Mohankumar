01

What to say

Open with the sentence, not the company: “Every business relationship starts with one question — can I trust who I'm dealing with?” Then: we are building the infrastructure that answers it. Do not open with features. Do not say “KYC”, “KYB” or “BGV” in the first two minutes — those words will anchor the investor into a commoditised category and you will spend the rest of the meeting climbing out.

Why it matters

The single biggest risk in this pitch is being mentally filed as “another verification vendor”. Category framing is decided in the first 60 seconds. The words that must land early are infrastructure, orchestration and network.

“So you're a KYC company?”

No — and the distinction is the whole business. KYC and KYB companies sell checks. We sell the layer above them: the policy that decides what needs checking, the orchestration that routes it, the evidence record that survives an audit, and the monitoring that keeps it true. We buy from those companies; we don't compete with them. Slide 5 shows exactly where we sit.

05

What to say

Slow down here. This is the slide that decides whether you are a vendor or infrastructure. Say it plainly: “We are not trying to out-verify AuthBridge or IDfy. We buy from companies like them.” Then trace the stack top to bottom with a finger. The investor should leave with one sentence: BID sits above the providers.

Why it matters

It defuses the two hardest objections at once — “this is crowded” and “the incumbents will crush you”. If BID is a customer of the incumbents rather than a rival, incumbency stops being a threat and starts being supply.

“Why won't the existing BGV/KYB providers just build this themselves?”

Some will try. Three things make it hard. First, culture: API and services businesses are optimised for volume and price per call, not for enterprise workflow depth — moving up-stack means a different product organisation and a different sales motion. Second, neutrality: a provider that builds an orchestration layer will always route to itself, and buyers know it, so the layer is worth less in their hands than in ours. Third, the network: our reuse advantage comes from entities verified across many buyers, which no single provider can assemble without the buyer-side relationships. That said — I would not claim this is a durable technical moat. It is a head start, and slide 18 is honest about which parts compound and which do not.

“What happens if a provider cuts you off or raises prices?”

It is a real dependency and we treat it as a first-order risk. Mitigation is structural: at least two qualified routes for every check that a signed customer policy marks as required, a router that fails over automatically, and a cost ledger per call so we see margin erosion the week it starts rather than at quarter end. The deeper commercial point is that we must secure caching and reuse rights in every provider contract — if reuse is contractually forbidden, our margin-expansion path disappears. That is the single most important term in our supplier agreements, and we negotiate it from the first contract, not the tenth.

Modelling “vendor” and “customer” as entity types. The same company then exists as three records, reuse becomes impossible, and the network effect can never be built.

The economics of density

The second time an entity is verified, most attributes are already current and disclosable under consent. Marginal provider cost falls sharply. Price does not fall proportionally. That gap is the business.

The honest constraint

A network is worth nothing until it is dense. We assume no network value for the first 12–18 months and the product must win standalone on workflow, evidence, audit trail and monitoring. Density beats breadth: 500 verified suppliers in one region and a few adjacent categories is worth more than 5,000 scattered nationally, because reuse only fires when two buyers want the same entity.

Where reuse actually applies

Business-registry checks reuse well across buyers. Manual person-side checks largely do not — for consent, validity and ethical reasons. The network effect is mostly a business-verification phenomenon, and we plan on that basis.

Why it matters

“How do you actually get network effects rather than just drawing them?”

Three preconditions, all measurable. One: vendor completion rate above 80%, or nothing downstream happens. If that third one comes back negative across the board, reuse collapses into an internal cost saving rather than a customer benefit — still valuable, but a materially different business, and we would tell you.

“What is your liability if a verification is wrong and a customer relies on it?”

It is a genuine exposure and we manage it three ways. Operationally: adverse findings are never auto-concluded — screening produces potential matches that require human disposition with a recorded reason. Commercially: bounded liability in contract plus professional indemnity cover. It is also on our open legal-items list — final terms need counsel before launch, and I would not want to give you a confident answer I cannot stand behind.

What to say

Lead with the two before/after pairs — they make an abstract principle concrete in five seconds. Then use the DPDP dates. Most founders say “privacy regulation is a tailwind” vaguely; you have a notification date, two commencement dates and a penalty ceiling. That specificity is the credibility.

Why it matters

“How do you handle privacy, really?”

Minimum necessary disclosure enforced at the authorization layer, not by policy. Every relationship carries a stated purpose, every disclosure beyond the public tier requires a specific, time-boxed, revocable consent artifact, and a person can see and dispute anything said about them. Our working position is that we are a processor for buyer-instructed verification and a fiduciary for the reusable profile — we build to the stricter reading, because dual-role compliance is expensive to retrofit and cheap to design in. The exact classification is on our legal open-items list.

“Doesn't the network model require sharing personal data across buyers?”

No, and this is a boundary we will not move. Business-entity data reuses across buyers under consent. A person's background result does not — it goes to the requesting employer under its stated purpose only, is never publicly resolvable, and is never reusable without fresh consent. That does constrain the network effect on the people side, and we plan on that basis rather than pretending otherwise.

This is not a technology problem, it is a trust and relationship problem in a specific regulatory geography. Most likely outcome is we become their verification layer rather than their competitor. Their ROI on building Indian data-source depth and provider orchestration is poor if we are already embedded, and integration is our stated strategy — we are not trying to be Ariba.

Appendix

Hard questions.

The fifteen questions most likely to decide this investment — with the answers we actually believe, including where we are uncertain.

1 · Isn't this market crowded?
Crowded with specialists, empty at the orchestration layer. The specialists are our suppliers. Our real incumbent is a spreadsheet, and it wins most deals today.
2 · Why won't IDfy, Signzy or AuthBridge build this?
Some will try. Moving up-stack requires enterprise workflow depth and a different sales motion; and a provider-owned orchestration layer is worth less to buyers because it will always route to itself. It is a head start, not a permanent barrier.
3 · What authority do you have to verify?
None, and we never claim any. We are a private platform. Authority comes from transparency — what was checked, from which source, by what method, when, to what scope, and how fresh.
4 · Why would an invited company join?
Their customer asked, and the alternative is a document pack over email. Reuse is the durable pull. Completion rate is the metric that proves it, and we have no data yet.
5 · Why would they ever pay?
They never pay to be verified. They pay when they switch sides and want to verify their own counterparties — a different budget, and a product they have already personally used.
6 · How do you avoid becoming a BGV company?
By never leading with BGV. It is price-driven, thin-margin and does not seed the network. We enter it from inside an existing vendor-verification account, on workflow and transparency rather than price.
7 · What is the moat, honestly?
Today, none — we have a head start. Over time: evidence history, reusable credentials, embedded integrations and the verification graph. We separate advantages from moats deliberately.
8 · What if reuse is contractually forbidden?
Then margin expansion disappears and we become a pass-through reseller. It is our single most important supplier term and we negotiate it from the first contract, not the tenth.
9 · Enterprise sales cycles will kill you.
Which is why we sell mid-market first, charge for pilots so they have an internal owner, land in one function, and expand into adjacent budgets rather than renegotiating.
10 · Are your margins real?
Modelled, not measured. Every provider cost is a placeholder. Two lines already fall below our 70% target under current assumptions and we can tell you exactly which and why.
11 · How do you handle false positives?
Screening produces potential matches, never conclusions. Human disposition is mandatory with a recorded reason, dismissed matches are remembered, and any entity can dispute a finding about itself.
12 · What is your liability if you get it wrong?
We report evidence with source, method, timestamp and scope — not conclusions — so the decision and the record stay with the customer. Plus bounded contractual liability and indemnity cover. Final terms need counsel.
13 · Doesn't the network need personal data to work?
No. Business data reuses under consent; a person's background result does not, ever. That genuinely limits network effects on the people side and we plan accordingly.
14 · How does this scale technically?
Event-driven and async by default, because issuer and human checks take days. Tenant isolation enforced in the data layer. The hard scaling problem is operations — exception handling and assisted onboarding — not compute.
15 · What kills this company?
A breach of background or relationship data, or a well-capitalised specialist moving up-stack before we reach density. The first is an engineering and governance problem; the second is a speed problem. Both are why this round exists.
16 · What would make you shut it down?
If pilots show vendor completion below roughly 50% and it cannot be moved, or if provider economics make ₹499-class pricing structurally loss-making at volume. Both are testable within two quarters.
A1

How to use this appendix

Do not present it. Keep it behind the close and jump to it when a question lands, which signals you anticipated it. Questions 8, 10 and 16 are the ones that most impress experienced investors, because each concedes something real — and a founder who volunteers a kill condition is far more credible than one who claims there isn't one.

The general rule for the Q&A

Where you have data, give it. Where you have a model, say “modelled”. Where you have neither, say so and name the date by which you will. The fastest way to lose an experienced investor is a confident answer to a question you have not actually tested — they will find out in diligence, and then everything else you said is suspect too.
